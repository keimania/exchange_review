1. Japan (FSA/JFSA + JPX)

1.1 Regulatory Authority Structure

Japan’s securities market regulation follows a multi-layered structure comprising a government regulator, a surveillance commission, and exchange-level self-regulation.

JFSA was established in 2000 (reorganized from the Financial Supervisory Agency, originally created in 1998) and exercises comprehensive regulatory authority over securities markets, banking, and insurance. The JFSA sets policy, drafts legislation, issues licenses, and supervises compliance. It reports directly to the Prime Minister and the Cabinet.

SESC was established in 1992 as an independent commission within the JFSA (structurally analogous to the relationship between the SEC’s Division of Enforcement and the full Commission in the US). The SESC conducts: - Market surveillance for insider trading, market manipulation, and other unfair trading practices - Inspections of financial instruments business operators (broker-dealers, asset managers) - Investigations of disclosure violations - Recommendations for administrative sanctions and criminal referrals to prosecutors

As of February 2026, the SESC continues to expand its enforcement capabilities, including proposed authority over crypto-asset insider trading under forthcoming FIEA amendments.

[Source: SESC, “Overview”, published February 2026, accessed 2026-04-06]

JPX-R (Japan Exchange Regulation, Inc.) was established as a wholly-owned subsidiary of Japan Exchange Group to serve as the dedicated self-regulatory organization for TSE and Osaka Exchange (OSE). JPX-R is described as the “quality control center” of the exchanges and performs: - Listing examinations: Reviews applications for new listings and continued listing compliance - Listed company compliance: Monitors corporate disclosure, governance compliance, and timely disclosure obligations - Trading participant examination and inspection: Examines and inspects broker-dealers (trading participants) for compliance with exchange rules - Market surveillance: Real-time monitoring of trading activity for manipulation, insider trading, and other anomalies; as of April 2025, JPX-R has introduced AI-powered market surveillance tools

[Source: JPX, “Outline of Self-Regulatory Operations”, accessed 2026-04-06; Mondovisione, “Japan Exchange Regulation Annual Report 2025”, August 2025]

Financial Instruments and Exchange Act (FIEA / éèåååå¼æ³)

The FIEA (Act No. 25 of 1948, substantially revised in 2006) is the principal statute governing Japan’s securities markets. Originally enacted as the Securities and Exchange Act (modeled on the US Securities Act of 1933 and Securities Exchange Act of 1934), it was comprehensively reformed and renamed the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act in 2006 (effective September 2007) to broaden its scope to cover a wider range of financial instruments beyond traditional securities.

[Source: JFSA, “Financial Instruments and Exchange Act”, accessed 2026-04-06]

2024 FIEA Amendments (Effective May 1, 2026)

Major amendments enacted in 2024 include:

  • Tender Offer Bid (TOB) threshold: Lowered from one-third to 30% for mandatory tender offers
  • “30% Rule” exemption: Small-scale purchases increasing shareholding by less than 0.5% are exempt from TOB, provided no acquisitions occurred in the previous six months
  • Large shareholding reporting clarifications: Scope of “joint holders” and “acts of material proposal” clarified; format of reports modernized through EDINET updates
  • Crypto-asset reclassification: JFSA proposed bringing crypto-assets under FIEA oversight (as “Type 1 Financial Instruments”), with legislative drafting targeted for 2026

[Source: Funds-Axis, “Japan’s 2024 FIEA Amendments”, accessed 2026-04-06; DLA Piper, “Recent developments on TOB rules and shareholding transparency in Japan”, January 2026]

1.3 Market Structure Rules

Trading Regulations: - Order types: Limit orders, market orders, and various conditional orders processed through the arrowhead trading engine - Price limits: Daily price limits (å¤å¹ å¶é) based on the previous day’s closing price; limits range from +/-30 yen (for stocks under 100 yen) to +/-7,000 yen (for stocks at 30,000-50,000 yen level), with wider limits for higher-priced stocks - Tick sizes: Multi-tiered based on price level and index membership (TOPIX 500 constituents have finer ticks) - Trading unit: Standardized at 100 shares for all listed stocks since October 2018 - Closing auction: Introduced November 2024 with a 5-minute pre-closing period (15:25-15:30) followed by Itayose matching

Listing Requirements: Governed by TSE listing rules and examined by JPX-R. Three main market segments (Prime, Standard, Growth) established in April 2022 market restructuring, plus TOKYO PRO Market for professional investors.

1.4 Market Surveillance

Japan employs a two-tier surveillance model:

JPX-R (First Line): - Real-time surveillance of all trading on TSE and OSE - AI-powered detection systems (introduced April 2025) for identifying suspicious trading patterns - Investigation of potential rule violations by trading participants and listed companies - Referral of suspected violations to SESC

SESC (Second Line): - Conducts formal investigations upon referral from JPX-R or on its own initiative - Authority to recommend administrative monetary penalties (èª²å¾´é) to JFSA - Criminal referrals to public prosecutors for serious violations - Inspection of financial instruments business operators - Cross-border coordination with overseas regulators - In Q4 2024, the SESC investigated inappropriate sharing of customer information between banks and securities affiliates

[Source: JPX, “JPX-R Inspection Plan for FY2025”, March 2025; SESC overview, February 2026]

1.6 Short Selling Regulations

Japan maintains a permanent regulatory framework for short selling under the FIEA and its Enforcement Order:

Rule Details
Naked short selling ban Permanent prohibition (originally a temporary crisis measure in 2008, made permanent); sellers must secure securities before selling short
Uptick rule (trigger-based) Price restriction activated when a stock’s price drops 10% or more from the base price; once triggered, short sales may only be executed at a price above the last traded price; restriction lasts until the end of the next trading day
Marking requirement All short sale orders must be flagged/marked as short sales
Position reporting Positions >= 0.2% must be reported to TSE; >= 0.5% publicly disclosed
Aggregation Short positions calculated on a per-investor, per-issue basis

[Source: JPX, “Short Selling Restrictions”, accessed 2026-04-06; Clifford Chance, “Significant changes to the Japanese short selling regulations”, 2013]

1.7 Foreign Investor Access

Japan’s securities market is generally open to foreign investors with no major restrictions on portfolio investment:

  • No pre-registration requirement for general portfolio investment in listed securities
  • No foreign ownership limits on most listed companies (sector-specific restrictions apply under FEFTA for national security sectors such as defense, nuclear energy, telecommunications)
  • Free repatriation of capital and profits

2025 FEFTA Amendments (effective May 19, 2025): - New “Specified Foreign Investors” classification for investors with obligations to disclose information to foreign governments (particularly targeting entities linked to foreign intelligence services) - Specified Foreign Investors must file prior notification when acquiring >= 1% of listed shares, with no eligibility for exemptions - Enhanced screening for investments in designated sensitive sectors

[Source: Lexology, “Japan’s 2025 FEFTA Amendments”, accessed 2026-04-06; Norton Rose Fulbright, “Global rules on foreign direct investment: Japan”, accessed 2026-04-06]

1.8 Corporate Governance Code and Stewardship Code

Corporate Governance Code (ã³ã¼ãã¬ã¼ãã¬ããã³ã¹ã»ã³ã¼ã)

First introduced in June 2015 and revised in June 2018 and June 2021, the Corporate Governance Code is a principles-based framework jointly published by the JFSA and TSE. It applies on a “comply or explain” basis.

Key Principles: - Board independence: At least one-third independent outside directors for Prime Market companies (majority encouraged) - Cross-shareholding: Companies should disclose rationale and reduce strategic cross-shareholdings; major insurance companies committed to full divestment - Cost of capital awareness: TSE’s January 2023 initiative requesting all Prime and Standard Market companies to disclose plans for improving cost of capital and stock price (PBR initiative); as of March 2025, over 90% of Prime Market companies have disclosed such plans

2025-2026 Revision Process: - JPX and JFSA convened the Expert Panel on the Revision of the Corporate Governance Code (third meeting held April 3, 2026) - Key topics under review include: further strengthening board effectiveness, sustainability disclosure, engagement with shareholders, and cross-shareholding unwinding

[Source: JPX, “Follow-up Council”, accessed 2026-04-06; Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance, “Tokyo Stock Exchange Initiative on Cost of Capital”, October 2025]

Stewardship Code (Version 3.0, June 26, 2025)

Japan’s Stewardship Code was first introduced in February 2014 and revised in May 2017 and March 2020. Version 3.0 was published on June 26, 2025.

Key Changes in Version 3.0: - Emphasis on moving from “form to substance” â discouraging box-ticking approaches - Beneficial shareholder transparency: Institutional investors should be prepared to disclose shareholding details to investee companies upon request (Guidance 4-2) - Collaborative engagement: Principle 4.5 encourages joint investor engagement to address governance and sustainability challenges - Principles-based flexibility: Recognition that sustainable value creation cannot be reduced to formulaic approaches

[Source: JFSA, “Revision of the Stewardship Code”, June 26, 2025; ACGA, “Decoding Japan’s New Stewardship Code and 2025 FSA Action Programme”, accessed 2026-04-06]

2. Hong Kong (SFC + HKEX)

2.1 Regulatory Authority Structure

Hong Kong operates under a dual regulatory model combining a statutory regulator (SFC) and exchange-based regulation through HKEX’s Listing Division.

[Source: SFC, “About the SFC”, accessed 2026-04-06]

SFC was established in 1989 following the 1987 stock market crash. It is an independent statutory body funded by transaction levies and fees. The SFC’s functions include: - Licensing and supervising intermediaries (10 types of regulated activities, including Type 1 â dealing in securities, Type 4 â advising on securities, Type 9 â asset management) - Administering the Securities and Futures Ordinance (SFO) - Market surveillance and enforcement - Regulating collective investment schemes - Approving listing rules and exercising oversight of HKEX’s listing function

HKEX Listing Division acts as the front-line regulator for listed companies under authority delegated by the SFC. The Listing Committee (comprising market practitioners and independent members) makes listing decisions. The SFC retains the power to: - Object to listing applications - Direct HKEX to amend listing rules - Exercise “dual filing” oversight of listing documents

The SFO (enacted 2002, effective April 1, 2003) is the principal legislation governing Hong Kong’s securities and futures markets. It consolidated 10 former ordinances into a single comprehensive statute.

2.3 Market Structure and Licensing

Market Structure: - Main Board: Premier market for established companies (HK$500 million minimum market cap at IPO) - GEM (Growth Enterprise Market): Growth board for smaller companies - Chapter 18A (2018): Pre-revenue biotech companies - Chapter 18B (2022): SPAC listings (minimum HK$1 billion IPO proceeds; de-SPAC within 36 months) - Chapter 18C (2023): Specialist Technology Companies (commercial: HK$4 billion min cap after September 2024 temporary reduction from HK$6 billion; pre-commercial: HK$8 billion, reduced from HK$10 billion, valid until August 2027) - Chapter 19C: Secondary listings of qualifying overseas-listed companies

Licensing: All intermediaries conducting regulated activities must be licensed by the SFC. Firms are subject to ongoing capital requirements, conduct rules, and regular examinations.

Stock Connect is a landmark cross-border market access scheme linking Hong Kong with the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges.

[Source: HKEX, “Stock Connect”, accessed 2026-04-06; IFEC, “Trading rules and arrangement”, accessed 2026-04-06]

2.5 Market Surveillance

SFC Market Surveillance: - Conducts real-time monitoring of trading activity across all SEHK-listed securities - Investigates suspected insider dealing, market manipulation, false trading, and other market misconduct - In Q3 2024, the SFC conducted 468 investigations spanning corporate disclosure, insider dealing, intermediary misconduct, and market manipulation - Dual enforcement routes: Market misconduct can be prosecuted through either (a) the Market Misconduct Tribunal (civil route) or (b) criminal prosecution before the courts

Recent Enforcement Highlights (2025): - In July 2025, joint SFC-ICAC operation targeted a market manipulation syndicate involving listed company executives; searches at 14 locations, multiple arrests - SFC probed alleged insider dealing involving staff at HKEX and SFC themselves, concerning leaks about dozens of listed companies [Source: Bloomberg, September 2025] - Market Sounding Guidelines took effect in May 2025, requiring licensed persons to implement information barrier protocols during market soundings (block trades, private placements)

[Source: SFC, “Regulatory update”, accessed 2026-04-06; A&O Shearman, “Hong Kong White-Collar/Financial Crime”, 2025]

2.7 Foreign Investor Access

Hong Kong operates one of the most open securities markets in Asia:

  • No pre-registration requirement for foreign investors
  • No foreign ownership limits on Hong Kong-listed securities (mainland China A-shares via Stock Connect are subject to aggregate and daily quotas)
  • Free capital movement: No foreign exchange controls; HKD freely convertible
  • Tax advantages: No capital gains tax, no dividend withholding tax on Hong Kong-listed securities, stamp duty of 0.1% per side (reduced from 0.13% in November 2023)
  • Settlement: T+2 for CCASS (Central Clearing and Settlement System) securities

3. Singapore (MAS + SGX)

3.1 Regulatory Authority Structure

Singapore adopts an integrated regulatory model where the central bank (MAS) serves as the single financial regulator, with SGX RegCo providing exchange-level self-regulation.

Authority Full Name Role
MAS Monetary Authority of Singapore Integrated financial regulator (central bank + securities regulator + banking regulator + insurance regulator); licensing, supervision, enforcement, monetary policy
SGX RegCo SGX Regulation (SGX RegCo Pte. Ltd.) Independent regulatory subsidiary of SGX Group; listing regulation, market surveillance, member supervision
CAD Commercial Affairs Department, Singapore Police Force Criminal investigation of financial crimes (works jointly with MAS)

[Source: MAS, “About MAS”, accessed 2026-04-06]

MAS is unique among major financial regulators as it combines central banking functions with securities regulation, banking supervision, and insurance oversight in a single institution. Established in 1971, MAS administers the Securities and Futures Act (SFA) and has authority to: - License and supervise capital markets services (CMS) license holders - Investigate and enforce market misconduct - Approve exchange and clearing house rules - Issue prohibition orders against individuals - Impose civil penalties for market abuse

SGX RegCo was established in 2016 and began operations in 2018 as an independent regulatory subsidiary of SGX Group. It maintains operational independence from SGX’s commercial functions and is governed by a Regulatory Conflicts Committee. SGX RegCo’s functions include: - Reviewing listing applications and administering listing rules for Mainboard and Catalist - Monitoring ongoing compliance of listed companies - Conducting market surveillance - Supervising trading members (securities and derivatives)

[Source: SGX, “About SGX RegCo”, accessed 2026-04-06]

3.2 Key Legislation

Securities and Futures Act 2001 (SFA)

The SFA (Act 42 of 2001, as amended) is Singapore’s principal legislation governing capital markets. It was significantly amended in 2005 and 2012, with further amendments ongoing.

[Source: MAS, “Securities and Futures Act 2001”, accessed 2026-04-06; Singapore Statutes Online, sso.agc.gov.sg]

3.3 Market Structure and Licensing

Market Segments: - Mainboard: Primary market for established companies; quantitative entry criteria including profit track record and/or market capitalization; minimum 25% public float - Catalist: Sponsor-supervised growth board for SMEs; no quantitative entry criteria set by SGX (sponsor determines suitability) - GlobalQuote: For secondary listings of foreign companies - Global Listing Board (proposed): Announced November 19, 2025 â a new dual-listing bridge between SGX and Nasdaq for companies with market cap above SGD 2 billion

[Source: MAS, “MAS Proposes Legislative and Regulatory Changes to Facilitate Dual Listings on Global Listing Board”, January 2026]

Global Listing Board â Key Proposed SFA Amendments (consultation closed February 8, 2026): - Single prospectus for dual listings on SGX and Nasdaq - Aligned IPO timelines between US and Singapore - Safe harbours for forward-looking statements, share repurchases, and pre-determined trades - Retail investors permitted to engage earlier in the IPO process

3.4 Market Surveillance

MAS-SGX Joint Surveillance Framework:

MAS and SGX RegCo operate a coordinated surveillance framework: - SGX RegCo conducts first-line real-time market surveillance using automated systems to detect unusual trading patterns - MAS conducts second-line oversight and formal enforcement investigations - MAS-SGX Trade Surveillance Practice Guide: Published jointly by MAS and SGX, provides guidance on trade surveillance practices for intermediaries

Enforcement Actions (2025): - In Q4 2025, MAS opened 163 review and investigation cases, with 58 cases pertaining to market misconduct (insider trading and false trading) - Civil penalty of S$50,000 imposed on Tan Tee Beng for insider trading in Tee International and Tee Land shares - Civil penalty of S$137,000 imposed on Ang Yew Jin Eugene for insider trading in Alpha Energy Holdings shares - Criminal charges filed against Xie Jianfeng for insider trading and deception under the SFA (October 2025)

Enforcement Priorities (2025-2026): MAS outlined enforcement priorities including: market misconduct (insider trading, false trading), intermediary misconduct, and technology-related risks.

3.6 Foreign Investor Access

Singapore maintains one of the most open capital markets in Asia:

  • No pre-registration requirement for foreign investors
  • No foreign ownership limits on SGX-listed securities (sector-specific restrictions may apply under other legislation, e.g., banking, media)
