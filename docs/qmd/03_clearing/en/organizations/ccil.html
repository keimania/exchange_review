1. Overview & Profile

Item Details
Official Name The Clearing Corporation of India Limited
Abbreviation CCIL
Country / City India / Mumbai
Website https://www.ccilindia.com
Year Established April 2001; operations commenced February 2002
Ownership Promoted by State Bank of India (SBI); shareholders include major Indian banks, primary dealers, and financial institutions [Source: CCIL Promoters/Shareholders]
Paid-up Preference Share Capital Rs 50 crore (5 crore 8.5% Redeemable Non-Convertible Cumulative Preference Shares) [Source: CCIL Company Profile]
Net Worth Rs 5,903 crore (as of 30 September 2024) [Source: ICRA Rating Report Feb 2025]
Regulatory Authority Reserve Bank of India (RBI) – authorized under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007
QCCP Status Yes – granted by RBI (2014) based on compliance with PFMI standards [Source: Global Custodian]
PFMI Compliance Designated as critical Financial Market Infrastructure (FMI) by RBI in July 2013; publishes PFMI qualitative and quantitative disclosures [Source: CCIL PFMI Disclosures]
Credit Rating AAA (Stable) by ICRA and CARE [Source: CARE Rating Mar 2025]
Subsidiary ClearCorp Dealing Systems (India) Ltd. – develops and operates trading platforms (NDS-OM, TREPS, FX-CLEAR, etc.)

Comparison with KRX/KSD: CCIL is a specialized CCP focused on fixed income, forex, and money markets – it does not clear equities or exchange-traded derivatives. This contrasts with KRX, which serves as CCP for all exchange-traded products (equities, derivatives). India’s equity clearing is handled separately by NSCCL (NSE), ICCL (BSE), and MCX-CCL (commodity derivatives).

Key Historical Milestones

Year Event
2001 (Apr) CCIL incorporated; promoted by SBI and major Indian banks
2002 (Feb) Commenced clearing and settlement of government securities transactions
2002 (Nov) Forex clearing segment launched (USD/INR interbank spot and forward transactions)
2003 Received INFINET membership for funds settlement through RBI’s RTGS system
2004 Collateralized Borrowing and Lending Obligation (CBLO) segment launched
2005 Rupee Derivatives (IRS/FRA) clearing commenced
2007 Authorized as payment system under Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007
2008 CDS segment and forex forwards/swaps clearing introduced
2013 (Jul) Designated as critical FMI by RBI for oversight under PFMI framework

2023 (Apr) ESMA derecognition took effect – European banks faced higher capital charges for CCIL exposures
2026 ESMA-RBI MoU signed, enabling CCIL to re-apply for EMIR recognition [Source: Angel One]

2. Clearing Model

2.1 CCP Services

CCIL operates across multiple market segments, each with distinct clearing mechanisms:

Segment Products Settlement Cycle Launch
Government Securities (G-Sec) Government bonds, T-Bills, State Development Loans, sovereign gold bonds T+1 (outright); T+0 to T+2 (repo) 2002
TREPS (Tri-party Repo) Collateralized money market borrowing/lending (replaced CBLO in 2018) T+0 to T+1 2018
Forex Settlement USD/INR spot, cash, tom; interbank forex transactions T+0 / T+1 / T+2 (spot) 2002
Forex Forwards USD/INR forwards, swaps Per contract maturity 2008
Rupee Derivatives OTC Interest Rate Swaps (IRS) and Forward Rate Agreements (FRA) referenced to MIBOR, MIOIS, MIFOR, Modified MIFOR; tenors up to 10 years Per contract maturity 2005
CDS Credit Default Swaps [unverified – limited activity] Per contract terms 2008

[Source: CCIL Company Profile; NISM - Role of CCIL]

Volume Breakdown (approximate FY2022 proportions): - TREPS: ~40% of total turnover - Government Securities: ~30% - Forex: ~30%

[Source: ICRA Rating Report]

2.3 Netting

CCIL employs different netting mechanisms across its segments:

Segment Netting Method
G-Sec (Outright) Multilateral netting – all trades due for settlement on the same day are netted to arrive at a single net obligation per member per security
TREPS Multilateral netting of funds along with outright and market repo trades
Forex (Spot) Multilateral netting of USD and INR legs separately; net USD and net INR positions determined per member
Rupee Derivatives Bilateral netting of cashflows; novation upon trade acceptance
  • For all guaranteed settlement segments, CCIL interposes itself as CCP through novation – the original bilateral trade is replaced by two contracts, one between CCIL and each counterparty
  • Netting significantly reduces settlement exposure; in the forex segment, netting efficiency typically reduces gross obligations by 80-90% [unverified]

[Source: CCIL Securities Settlement FAQ; CCIL Forex Settlement]

2.4 Clearing Membership

CCIL has segment-specific membership:

Member Type Eligible Entities
G-Sec Segment Scheduled commercial banks, primary dealers, insurance companies, pension funds, mutual funds, FPIs
Forex Segment Authorized dealer banks (AD Category I)
Rupee Derivatives Market Makers (scheduled banks, standalone primary dealers, AIFIs); Non-Retail Entities; Retail Entities (corporates, FPIs)
TREPS Banks, primary dealers, mutual funds, insurance companies, corporates
  • All members must meet minimum net worth requirements and contribute to the Default Fund
  • Members must maintain adequate collateral with CCIL for margin and settlement purposes
  • With effect from November 2023, collateral contribution to the Securities Segment SGF became segment-agnostic (common pool)

[Source: CCIL Rupee Derivatives; CCIL Collateral Management]

3. Risk Management

3.1 Margin Framework

CCIL applies a comprehensive margin framework across all segments:

Margin Type Description
Initial Margin (IM) Covers potential future exposure (PFE); calculated using VaR-based models with a confidence level of 99% and appropriate lookback/holding periods
Mark-to-Market (MTM) Margin Covers current exposure based on daily (or intraday) revaluation of open positions
Volatility Margin Additional margin charged during periods of heightened market volatility
Concentration Margin Charged when a member’s positions are disproportionately large relative to the market
Spread Margin Applied to spread positions in the Rupee Derivatives segment

[Source: CCIL Risk Management Introduction]

Collateral Types Accepted: - Cash (INR and USD depending on segment) - Government of India securities and Treasury Bills - Since November 2023, collateral can be posted to a segment-agnostic common pool

Comparison with KRX: KRX uses a SPAN-based margin system for derivatives and a VaR-based system for equities, with similar margin types (initial margin, variation margin, additional margin). CCIL’s segment-specific margin models reflect the OTC/wholesale nature of its markets, while KRX’s models are designed for exchange-traded retail/institutional markets.

3.3 Default Waterfall

CCIL maintains a default waterfall structure to absorb member default losses:

Layer Resource Amount (approx.)
1 Defaulting member’s margins (IM, MTM, volatility margin) Varies per member
2 Defaulting member’s Default Fund contribution Varies per member
3 CCIL’s own capital – “Skin in the Game” (Settlement Reserve Fund, SRF) Rs 2,600 crore (as of 31 Mar 2024)
4 Non-defaulting members’ Default Fund contributions (loss mutualization) Part of Rs 11,928 crore total DF (as of 30 Sep 2024)
5 Additional assessment on surviving members As per CCIL’s Bye-laws, Rules and Regulations

[Source: CCIL PFMI Qualitative Disclosures Mar 2024; ICRA Rating Report Feb 2025]

Default Fund Structure: - CCIL maintains six separate Default Funds across its segments - Total Default Fund: Rs 11,928 crore (as of 30 September 2024) - Members contribute cash and eligible Government of India securities / Treasury Bills - Default Fund adequacy is regularly stress-tested

[Source: CCIL Default Fund]

Comparison with KRX: KRX’s default waterfall similarly follows the sequence of defaulter’s margin -> defaulter’s clearing fund contribution -> KRX’s own capital -> non-defaulting members’ clearing fund contributions. CCIL’s SRF of Rs 2,600 crore (~USD 310 million) as skin-in-the-game is substantial for an emerging market CCP. KRX’s joint compensation fund follows a comparable mutualization principle.

3.6 Recovery & Resolution

  • CCIL is designated as a critical Financial Market Infrastructure (FMI) by RBI since July 2013
  • RBI acts as the oversight and resolution authority under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007
  • CCIL has recovery tools including:
    • Cash calls / additional assessment on members
    • Partial settlement / delayed settlement
    • Loss allocation to surviving members
    • Voluntary tear-up of positions [unverified]
  • India does not yet have a dedicated CCP resolution framework comparable to EU CCPRRR or US OLA, though RBI’s oversight framework provides de facto resolution powers [unverified]
  • CCIL’s systemic importance means RBI would likely intervene directly in a severe stress scenario

4. Settlement

4.1 Settlement Cycle

Segment Standard Cycle Settlement Mode
G-Sec (Outright, NDS-OM) T+1 DVP via RBI’s RTGS
G-Sec (Market Repo) T+0 to T+1 (first leg); various (second leg) DVP
TREPS T+0 (same day); T+1 Net settlement
Forex Spot T+2 PvP (Payment versus Payment)
Forex Cash T+0 PvP
Forex Tom T+1 PvP
Forex Forwards/Swaps Per contract maturity Net settlement
Rupee Derivatives (IRS) Per cashflow dates Net settlement of periodic cashflows

[Source: CCIL Securities Settlement FAQ; CCIL Forex Settlement]

4.2 DVP Model

Government Securities: - CCIL uses DVP Model 1 (gross securities against gross funds) for non-netted trades and DVP Model 3 (net securities against net funds) for netted trades through RBI’s RTGS system - Securities leg settles through RBI’s Securities Settlement System (SSS) / Public Debt Office (PDO) - Funds leg settles through RBI’s Real-Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) system - Settlement finality is achieved upon completion of both legs through RBI’s systems

Forex Settlement: - Uses a Payment versus Payment (PvP) mechanism for USD/INR settlement - INR leg settles through RBI’s RTGS - USD leg settles through CCIL’s USD settlement account with designated settlement banks - Netting of USD and INR obligations separately before settlement

[Source: CCIL PFMI Qualitative Disclosures; CCIL Guaranteed Settlement]

Comparison with KRX/KSD: KSD uses DVP Model 3 (net/net) for equity settlement via BOK-Wire+. CCIL’s use of DVP Model 1 for gross G-Sec trades reflects the wholesale, high-value nature of government bond transactions, while its netted settlement for TREPS and forex is similar in concept to KSD’s approach for equities.

5. Technology & Infrastructure

Platform / System Description Operated By
NDS-OM (Negotiated Dealing System - Order Matching) RBI-owned anonymous electronic trading platform for government securities; also used for OTC deal reporting ClearCorp (CCIL subsidiary)
NDS-CALL Electronic dealing platform for Call, Notice & Term Money market ClearCorp
FX-CLEAR Electronic dealing platform for interbank forex transactions ClearCorp
FX-SWAP Platform for forex swap transactions ClearCorp
TREPS (Tri-Party Repo Dealing System) Replaced CBLO platform; facilitates collateralized borrowing and lending ClearCorp
ASTROID Automated System for Tendering, Reporting, Operations, Information and Data Dissemination – auction system for G-Sec primary issuance ClearCorp
SARVAM Service for Analysis of Risk, Valuation and Margining – provides valuation, margining, collateral management, and risk analytics for Non-Centrally Cleared Derivatives (NCCDs) CCIL
F-TRAC Provides reference rates and valuations for fixed income and money market instruments CCIL

[Source: CCIL Company Profile; ClearCorp Company Profile]

Note: ClearCorp Dealing Systems (India) Ltd., a wholly-owned subsidiary of CCIL, develops and operates most of the trading platforms. This vertical integration of trading and clearing infrastructure is distinctive – it means CCIL (through ClearCorp) provides end-to-end infrastructure from trade execution to clearing and settlement for India’s wholesale financial markets.

6. Regulatory Framework

Aspect Details
Primary Regulator Reserve Bank of India (RBI)
Key Legislation Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007; RBI (Payment and Settlement Systems) Regulations, 2008
Authorization Authorized as a payment system by RBI under PSS Act, 2007
FMI Designation Designated as critical Financial Market Infrastructure by RBI (July 2013)
PFMI Oversight Supervised using CPSS-IOSCO PFMI framework; publishes qualitative and quantitative disclosures
QCCP Status Granted by RBI (2014) based on PFMI compliance
ESMA Recognition Lost EMIR third-country recognition in April 2023; ESMA-RBI MoU signed in 2026 to enable re-application
Governance Rules CCIL’s Bye-laws, Rules and Regulations (BRR) govern clearing operations, membership, risk management, and default procedures

ESMA Derecognition Issue: - In October 2022, ESMA refused recognition of CCIL under EMIR Article 25 due to the absence of a cooperation arrangement with RBI - The core dispute was ESMA’s demand for direct audit and inspection rights over CCIL, which RBI rejected as a sovereignty concern - Derecognition took effect in April 2023, forcing European banks to apply higher capital charges (non-QCCP treatment) for CCIL exposures - In 2026, ESMA and RBI signed an MoU resolving the impasse, enabling CCIL to re-apply for EMIR recognition

[Source: ESMA Press Release; ESMA-RBI MoU]

Comparison with KRX: KRX holds QCCP recognition in multiple jurisdictions. The CCIL-ESMA episode highlighted the tension between domestic regulatory sovereignty and international regulatory cooperation for CCPs – an issue that does not significantly affect KRX given Korea’s cooperative regulatory relationships with EU/US authorities.

7. Interoperability

Arrangement Details
RBI Systems Deep integration with RBI’s RTGS (funds settlement), SSS/PDO (securities settlement), and e-Kuber core banking system
CLS Bank India is not a member of CLS Bank for forex settlement; CCIL’s PvP mechanism serves as the domestic alternative
SWIFT Uses SWIFT messaging for certain interbank communications
International Links Limited direct CCP-to-CCP links; cross-border participation primarily through CCIL membership by foreign bank branches operating in India
  • CCIL does not have interoperability arrangements with other CCPs (unlike European CCPs that have CCP links for equity clearing)
  • Foreign participation in Indian G-Sec and forex markets (by FPIs, etc.) occurs through CCIL members (custodian banks), not through direct CCP links
  • The ESMA-RBI MoU (2026) may pave the way for deeper international integration

9. Recent Developments

Date Development
2023 (Nov) Securities Segment SGF collateral made segment-agnostic (common pool across segments)
2024 CCIL PFMI Quantitative Disclosures published for December 2024 [Source: CCIL PQD Dec 2024]
2025 (Sep) 39th Portfolio Compression cycle for MIBOR IRS: 27,622 out of 30,855 eligible trades terminated (89.4% compression rate) [Source: CCIL Derivatives Quarterly Q2 FY26]
2025 RBI proposed mandatory Unique Transaction Identifier (UTI) for all OTC rupee interest rate and foreign currency derivatives
2025 Standalone Primary Dealers authorized as AD Category-III for non-deliverable derivative contracts involving the Rupee
2026 ESMA-RBI MoU signed – CCIL can re-apply for EMIR recognition, resolving the 3+ year derecognition dispute
Ongoing SARVAM platform expansion for NCCD margining, collateral management, and risk analytics services
Ongoing CCIL Factbook 2024-25 published with comprehensive segment-wise data [Source: CCIL Factbook 2024-25]

Key Trends: - Resolution of ESMA recognition dispute will restore QCCP treatment for European bank exposures to CCIL - Continued expansion of OTC derivatives clearing scope (IRS, FRA, potentially FX options) - Portfolio compression exercises reducing outstanding notional in IRS market - SARVAM platform extending CCIL’s role beyond CCP into risk infrastructure for non-cleared derivatives - India’s G-Sec market inclusion in global bond indices (e.g., JP Morgan GBI-EM) driving increased foreign participation through CCIL’s clearing infrastructure
